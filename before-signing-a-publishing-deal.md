A publishing deal can be the best thing that happens to a game, or the reason it stalls. The difference usually sits in the contract. Many studios sign their first agreement under time pressure, focused on the headline revenue split, and only later discover what they gave away on rights, territories or termination.

> **Not legal advice:** This article gives general information only. Have a qualified lawyer review any publishing agreement before you sign it.

Below are the terms that matter most, what each one means in practice, and the questions worth asking before you commit.

## Publishing rights

The grant of rights defines what the publisher can actually do with your game. It may cover publishing, distribution, marketing, localisation, merchandising, sequels, ports and more.

Read this clause carefully. A broad grant can quietly include rights you never intended to license, such as derivative works or future titles in the same series. Ask for a narrow, specific grant that matches what the publisher will genuinely deliver.

## Exclusivity

Exclusivity means you cannot work with anyone else for the rights covered. It is common and often reasonable, since a publisher investing in marketing wants protection. The questions are scope and duration.

- Is exclusivity limited to certain territories or platforms?
- Does it apply only to this game, or to your future games too?
- Does it fall away if the publisher fails to launch or meet agreed targets?

## Territories

Territory clauses set where the publisher can sell the game. "Worldwide" is simple but may be more than a given partner can handle well. A partner strong in one region may add little elsewhere.

If you split territories between partners, define borders precisely, including how online stores that serve several countries will be handled. Also check who is responsible for regulatory compliance in each territory. In India, for example, the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 prohibits offering online money games and provides for an Authority on Online Gaming that can categorise and register games and platforms (SCC Times). Your contract should be clear about who manages obligations like these.

## Platforms

Platform clauses list where the game will appear: mobile stores, PC storefronts, consoles, web, cloud or OEM channels. Check that:

- Each platform listed is one the publisher will actually support
- Rights for platforms not yet in scope stay with you
- Responsibility for porting, certification and platform fees is clearly assigned

## Revenue sharing

The revenue split gets the most attention, but the definition of "revenue" often matters more than the percentage.

### Gross or net

Is the split calculated on gross receipts or on net revenue after deductions? If net, list every deduction allowed: store fees, taxes, refunds, payment processing, marketing spend, localisation costs. Unclear deductions can shrink your share considerably.

### Recoupment

Many deals let the publisher recover certain costs before you receive royalties. Agree exactly which costs are recoupable, whether there is a cap, and whether recoupment is limited to this game or can be taken from other titles you have with the same publisher (known as cross-collateralisation).

### Escalators

Some agreements adjust the split as the game succeeds. If yours does, make sure the thresholds are defined and measurable.

## Marketing expenditure

A publisher's value often lies in marketing. Yet many contracts say little about what will be spent. Ask for:

- A minimum marketing commitment, or at least a launch plan with a budget range
- Clarity on whether marketing spend is recoupable from your share
- Approval or consultation rights over key campaigns and how your studio is credited

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Studios that agree a written launch marketing plan before signing face fewer disputes after launch. This is a widely held view in the industry, not one we have measured.

## IP ownership

As a rule, you should keep ownership of your game's intellectual property, including code, art, characters and trademarks. The publisher receives a licence, not ownership.

Watch for clauses that transfer IP, give the publisher rights over sequels or spin-offs, or claim ownership of anything the publisher contributes, such as localised assets or marketing materials. If the publisher funds development, expect more negotiation here, and make sure any IP concession is matched by real value.

## Contract duration

The term sets how long the deal lasts. Look at:

- The initial term and whether it starts at signing or at launch
- Automatic renewal and how to opt out
- What happens to sales, player accounts and store listings at the end of the term
- Sell-off periods that let the publisher keep selling after expiry

A long term with no performance conditions can tie up your game even if the publisher loses interest.

## Reporting and audit

You cannot check a revenue share you cannot see. Ask for:

- Regular statements, monthly or quarterly, with a defined format
- Data broken down by territory, platform and revenue type
- Access to key performance data such as installs, retention and spend
- An audit right, and a rule that the publisher pays audit costs if a significant underpayment is found
- Clear payment timelines after each reporting period

## Termination

Termination clauses decide how you get out if things go wrong. Make sure you can terminate if the publisher:

- Fails to launch by an agreed date
- Misses payments or reports
- Becomes insolvent
- Breaches material obligations and does not fix them within a set period

Check what happens on termination: rights should revert to you promptly, along with store accounts, player data where lawful, and marketing assets you need to continue.

## Pre-signing checklist

- [ ] The grant of rights is limited to what the publisher will actually deliver
- [ ] Exclusivity is limited by territory, platform and time
- [ ] Territories and platforms are listed precisely
- [ ] "Revenue" is defined, with every allowed deduction named
- [ ] Recoupable costs are listed and capped, with no cross-collateralisation unless agreed
- [ ] There is a written marketing commitment or launch plan
- [ ] You keep ownership of your IP, including sequels
- [ ] The term, renewal and sell-off period are clear
- [ ] Reporting frequency, detail and audit rights are defined
- [ ] You can terminate for non-performance, and rights revert promptly
- [ ] Regulatory responsibility in each territory is assigned
- [ ] A qualified lawyer has reviewed the full agreement
