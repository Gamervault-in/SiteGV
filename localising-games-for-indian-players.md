Many studios treat localisation for India as a translation task: add Hindi, ship, move on. The result is often a game that is technically readable but still feels foreign. Indian players notice when a game was built for someone else. This article covers what localisation involves beyond the text file, and where studios most often go wrong.

## Start with who you are localising for

About 66% of Indian gamers are outside metros, and the female share of gamers rose from 41% to 44% in FY24. The typical player is not a metro-based, English-first professional. Localisation decisions should start from that player.

That does not mean every game needs every language on day one. It means making a deliberate choice about which players you want to reach first, and localising for them properly.

## Language choices

English is widely understood among urban players and in tech-heavy communities, but it is not enough for most of the audience. Hindi reaches a large share of players across the north and centre of the country. Other major regional languages matter enormously in their own states.

A practical approach:

- **Launch with English and Hindi** for the broadest initial reach.
- **Add regional languages in sequence**, guided by where your installs and retention actually come from.
- **Consider Hinglish** (Hindi and English mixed, often in Latin script) for casual tone, UI hints and marketing, where it can feel more natural than formal Hindi.
- **Do not machine-translate and ship.** Formal, dictionary-style Hindi can read as stiff or even comic in a game context.

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Conversational, mixed-language copy may outperform formal translation for casual and social games in India. Test variants with real players before committing to a single register.

## Cultural references

Culture shows up in small details: names, food, festivals, clothing, gestures, colours, jokes and the way characters address each other. A reference that is neutral in one market can be confusing or sensitive in another.

Areas to review:

- **Festivals and seasons**: Diwali, Holi, Eid, Christmas, Onam and regional new years can anchor events, but each has its own meaning and audience. Treat them with care, not as a theme skin.
- **Food and everyday objects**: swapping a burger for a regional snack is a small change that signals effort.
- **Religion and mythology**: Indian mythology is a rich source of themes, and also an area where careless treatment causes offence. Get local review for anything that touches deities, sacred places or religious symbols.
- **Sport**: cricket is a shared cultural language and a natural hook for events and cosmetics.
- **Humour**: wordplay rarely survives translation. Rewrite jokes rather than translate them.

## Characters and settings

Players respond to seeing themselves in a game. That does not require turning every title into an India-set game, but it helps to offer characters, skins and settings that feel familiar.

- **Characters**: offer a range of skin tones, body types, names and outfits that include Indian players without stereotyping them.
- **Settings**: a street market, a monsoon city or a cricket ground can make an event or map feel local.
- **Voice**: if your game uses voice, regional accents in English can feel more natural than a neutral international voice.

Avoid caricature. A turban, a sari and a curry joke do not amount to cultural localisation.

As a hypothetical example, a Scandinavian casual studio with a cooking game might add a festival-season kitchen, regional dishes and a set of characters with Indian names, while keeping the core loop unchanged. The game stays itself, but it now feels like it was made with Indian players in mind.

## UI adaptation

UI is where localisation often breaks.

- **Script rendering**: Devanagari and other Indian scripts need proper font support, including conjunct characters. Test on real devices, not only in the editor.
- **Text expansion**: some translations run longer than English. Leave room in buttons, labels and dialogue boxes.
- **Font size and legibility**: typical smartphones cost $200 to $400, and many have smaller or lower-resolution screens. Small text in complex scripts becomes unreadable quickly.
- **Numbers and currency**: show prices in rupees, and consider Indian number formatting (lakh and crore) where it fits the audience.
- **Icons over text**: where possible, rely on clear icons so players can navigate even before they read.

## Pricing and payment flows

Localisation includes the price tag and the checkout. A beautifully localised game with a card-only payment flow will still lose buyers.

The data is clear on payments: 83% of players prefer UPI or digital wallets. On pricing, payer conversion held steady at 25% in CY25, with mid-core ARPPU at $15 and casual at $3. Many players will pay, but in small amounts.

- **Set regional price points** rather than converting dollar prices.
- **Offer small, low-entry packs** so first purchases feel safe.
- **Make UPI the default path** and keep the number of steps low.
- **Localise the store copy for each offer**, so players understand exactly what they are buying.
- **Use rewarded ads as a fair alternative** for players who will not pay. 47% of users are ad-tolerant, and rewarded ads carried a $2.43 eCPM compared with $1.1 overall.

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Showing prices in rupees with familiar round numbers may increase first-purchase conversion more than discounts on dollar-based tiers. Compare both in a controlled test.

## Testing and feedback

Localisation is never right first time. Plan for iteration.

- **Native-speaker review** for every language, ideally from the regions you are targeting, not only from a translation vendor.
- **Device testing** on low and mid-range Android phones, since Android is about 90% of the market.
- **Small player panels** in metro and non-metro cities to check tone, humour and cultural fit.
- **In-game feedback routes** in local languages, so players can flag problems easily.
- **Segmented analytics**: compare retention and spend by language setting, not just by region.

## Localisation checklist

- [ ] Define the first player segments you are localising for
- [ ] Choose launch languages and a sequence for adding more
- [ ] Decide on register (formal, conversational, mixed) per language
- [ ] Review names, festivals, food, mythology and humour with local reviewers
- [ ] Add characters, skins or settings that feel familiar without stereotyping
- [ ] Test script rendering and text expansion on real devices
- [ ] Show prices in rupees with regional price points
- [ ] Put UPI and wallets at the centre of checkout
- [ ] Localise store listings, screenshots and ad creatives
- [ ] Run native-speaker and player-panel tests before scaling
