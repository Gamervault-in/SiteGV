A publishing deal can shape a game's success for years. The right partner brings reach, expertise and resources a studio could not build alone. The wrong one can lock up rights, slow decisions and leave a good game underperforming. This article sets out a practical framework for assessing publishing partners, and the questions worth asking before you sign anything.

## Start with what you actually need

Before comparing partners, be clear about your own gaps. Publishers offer different mixes of services, and paying for help you do not need is expensive.

Common needs include:

- Funding for development, porting or marketing
- Platform relationships and storefront visibility
- Marketing, community and creator outreach
- Localisation and quality assurance
- User acquisition for free-to-play games
- Regional knowledge for specific markets
- Analytics, live operations and monetisation support
- Legal, compliance and commercial administration

Rank these by importance. A studio with funding but no marketing experience needs a very different partner from one with a strong community but no budget.

## The assessment framework

### 1. Publishing experience

Look at what the partner has actually done, not what they say they can do.

- Which games have they published, and in which genres and platforms?
- How did those games perform after launch, not just at launch?
- Do they have experience with games of your size and budget?
- Who on their team will work on your game, and what have those people done before?

Experience in your genre matters. A partner who knows strategy games may not know how to market a narrative adventure.

If a partner is new and has no published titles yet, that is not automatically a reason to walk away, but you should weigh the experience of the individuals involved and protect yourself more carefully in the contract.

### 2. Distribution relationships

Distribution is about getting your game in front of players on the right platforms and in the right markets.

- Which storefronts and platforms do they work with directly?
- Can they help with featuring, promotions or platform events?
- Do they have relationships with regional stores, telecom operators or device makers where relevant?
- How do they handle certification and submissions?

Ask for specific examples. General claims of "strong relationships" are hard to test.

### 3. Marketing support

Marketing is often the main reason studios sign with a publisher.

- What will they spend on marketing, and is that commitment in the contract?
- Who decides how the budget is spent?
- Do they have in-house marketing, community and creator teams, or do they outsource?
- How do they approach pre-launch community building?
- What happens to marketing support after launch?

Be wary of vague commitments. A marketing plan with named activities, timings and budgets is far more useful than a promise to "support the launch".

### 4. Reporting and transparency

You need to know how your game is doing and where the money goes.

- How often will they share sales, revenue and player data?
- Will you have direct access to dashboards and storefront data?
- How are marketing and other costs reported?
- Do you have audit rights over their accounts for your game?
- How quickly are payments made after revenue is received?

Poor reporting is one of the most common sources of friction between studios and publishers.

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Studios that agree reporting formats and frequency before signing are likely to have fewer disputes later. Ask peers who have worked with the partner how reporting worked in practice.

### 5. Commercial terms

The headline revenue share is only part of the deal.

- **Revenue share:** What split applies, and does it change over time or at certain revenue levels?
- **Recoupment:** Which costs are recouped before you receive revenue? Is recoupment capped?
- **Advances:** Is any funding an advance against royalties or a separate investment?
- **Rights:** Which platforms, territories and time periods does the deal cover?
- **IP ownership:** Do you keep ownership of your game and its IP? What about sequels and spin-offs?
- **Exit terms:** Under what conditions can either side end the agreement, and what happens to the game then?
- **Upfront fees:** Does the partner charge any fees before launch?

Always have a lawyer with games industry experience review the contract.

### 6. Regional expertise

If you are targeting specific markets, local knowledge can make a large difference.

- Does the partner have people based in or experienced with your target markets?
- Do they understand local payment methods, pricing and player expectations?
- Can they handle local regulation, age ratings and compliance?
- Do they have local creator, media and community relationships?
- Can they localise to the right standard, not just translate?

A global publisher may be strong in some regions and weak in others. A regional specialist may go deeper in fewer markets. Choose based on where your players are.

## Warning signs

Be cautious if a partner:

- Pushes for a fast signature without time for legal review
- Asks for rights to all platforms, territories and future games without clear reason
- Will not commit to marketing spend or reporting standards in writing
- Cannot name the people who will work on your game
- Charges large upfront fees without clear deliverables
- Has a pattern of disputes with other studios

## Questions to ask checklist

- [ ] Which games similar to ours have you published, and how did they perform over time?
- [ ] Who exactly will work on our game, and what is their experience?
- [ ] Which platforms and storefronts do you work with directly?
- [ ] What marketing budget will you commit to in the contract?
- [ ] Who approves marketing decisions?
- [ ] How often will you report sales, revenue and costs, and in what format?
- [ ] Will we have direct access to storefront and analytics data?
- [ ] Which costs are recouped, and is recoupment capped?
- [ ] Which platforms, territories and time periods does the deal cover?
- [ ] Do we keep ownership of our IP, including sequels?
- [ ] How can either side end the agreement, and what happens to the game?
- [ ] What local expertise do you have in our target markets?
- [ ] Can we speak with studios you have worked with?

## Making the decision

Speak with more than one partner. Compare not only terms but also how each one engages with your game. A partner who asks thoughtful questions about your players, your design and your goals is more likely to be a good long-term fit than one who leads with a standard term sheet.

Trust your judgement about the people. A publishing relationship often runs for years, and you will need to work through problems together.
