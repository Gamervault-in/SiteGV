For many Indian studios, Southeast Asia looks like a natural next step. The region is mobile-heavy, price-sensitive and diverse, which describes the conditions Indian teams already build for. But "Southeast Asia" is not one market. It is a set of countries with different languages, payment habits, platform preferences and regulators. Treating it as a single launch region is one of the most common ways to waste a first international budget.

This article sets out how to think about market selection, adaptation, distribution, partners, monetisation, marketing and operations. We do not have verified market-size data for individual Southeast Asian countries, so this piece stays qualitative. Where we make an observation that needs checking against your own data, we flag it.

## Start from what Indian studios already do well

Indian studios have spent years designing for a demanding environment. In FY24, Android made up about 90% of India's gaming market, and a typical smartphone cost between $200 and $400 (Lumikai, via GamesBeat). Teams that ship well on that hardware have learned to manage install size, memory use, battery drain and patchy networks.

That experience is a genuine asset in parts of Southeast Asia where similar devices are common. It is not an automatic advantage, though. Local competitors also build for these conditions, and some regional players have deep distribution relationships that a new entrant will not.

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Device and network conditions in several Southeast Asian markets are close enough to India's that a well-optimised Indian build needs little technical rework. Test this with real device data from each target country before committing.

## Market selection

Pick one or two countries first, not the whole region. Useful questions to ask of each candidate:

- **Genre fit.** Which genres already succeed there? A genre that works in India may have strong incumbents, or no audience, elsewhere.
- **Platform mix.** Is the market mostly Android, or is there a meaningful iOS or PC audience that changes your build and pricing plans?
- **Payment behaviour.** How do players actually pay? Carrier billing, e-wallets, app store cards and bank transfers carry different weightings in different countries.
- **Language load.** Can you serve the market in English, or do you need full localisation from day one?
- **Regulatory clarity.** Are there content, data or age-rating rules that affect your game?
- **Partner availability.** Is there a credible local publisher, platform or distribution partner who knows your genre?

Score each country on these factors, with your own evidence, before you decide. A smaller market with a strong genre fit and a willing partner is often a better first move than a larger market where you would arrive unknown.

## Cultural adaptation

Localisation is more than translation. For a Southeast Asian launch, think in three layers.

### Language

Translate the interface, tutorials, store listing and support content. Use native-speaking translators who play games, then test in context. Text expansion and right-sized fonts matter, especially on small screens.

### Content and themes

Review art, characters, symbols, festivals and humour for local sensitivity. Some themes common in Indian games, such as mythology or cricket, may not carry the same meaning abroad. Equally, local festivals and holidays are strong live-ops moments if you plan for them early.

### Social and competitive features

Players in different markets use chat, guilds and leaderboards differently. Consider whether your social systems need regional servers, local moderation or adjusted matchmaking.

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Players in your target country respond better to locally themed seasonal events than to global ones. Run a small A/B test during soft launch rather than assuming.

## Distribution

Most Indian mobile studios will start with Google Play and the App Store. Beyond that, consider:

- **Regional Android stores and OEM channels.** Pre-installs and device-maker stores can reach players who rarely browse the main stores.
- **Telecom partnerships.** Carrier billing and bundles can lower the payment barrier for players without cards.
- **Platform and community partners.** Local streaming, social and messaging platforms can drive discovery.

Each channel has its own commercial terms, technical requirements and reporting. Do not add channels faster than you can support them.

## Choosing a publishing partner

A local or regional publishing partner can shorten the learning curve considerably. Look for:

- Proven experience in your genre, shown with titles you can check yourself
- Real local teams for marketing, community and support, not just a sales office
- Clear territory scope, so you know which countries they actually cover
- Transparent reporting on spend, installs and revenue
- Terms that let you keep your IP and recover rights if performance falls short

Ask to speak to studios they have worked with. A partner who resists that request is telling you something.

## Monetisation

Do not copy your Indian pricing. Use the app stores' local price tiers as a starting point, then test. Consider:

- **Local payment methods.** If players cannot pay easily, your conversion will suffer however good the offer is.
- **Hybrid models.** Rewarded ads alongside in-app purchases can serve players who will not pay while still rewarding those who do.
- **Offer design.** Starter packs, battle passes and subscriptions may perform differently by market.

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Ad-supported play will make up a larger share of revenue in your first Southeast Asian market than in India. Measure this in soft launch before building your forecast around it.

## Marketing

Southeast Asian audiences are often reached through creators, community groups and short video, alongside paid user acquisition. Practical steps:

- Work with local creators who already cover your genre
- Build community channels in the local language before launch
- Test creatives by country, since humour and visual style do not always travel
- Set separate acquisition cost targets for each market

## Operational considerations

Expansion adds ongoing work, not just a launch project. Plan for:

- **Live ops across time zones.** Events, maintenance windows and patch releases need to suit local peak hours.
- **Customer support.** Players expect help in their own language and on the channels they use.
- **Servers and latency.** Competitive and real-time games may need regional hosting.
- **Compliance.** Data protection, consumer protection and content rules vary by country. Get local advice.
- **Tax and payments.** Understand withholding tax, store fees and how and when revenue reaches you.

## Southeast Asia readiness checklist

- [ ] Shortlisted one or two countries using a written scoring of genre, platform, payments, language, regulation and partners
- [ ] Gathered device and network data for each target country
- [ ] Budgeted for full localisation, including store listing and support content
- [ ] Reviewed art, themes and events for cultural fit
- [ ] Mapped local payment methods and confirmed your game supports them
- [ ] Set local price tiers and a plan to test them
- [ ] Spoken to at least two potential partners and checked their references
- [ ] Planned live ops and support for local time zones
- [ ] Taken local advice on data, consumer and content rules
- [ ] Defined soft-launch success metrics before launch
