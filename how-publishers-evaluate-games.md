When a studio pitches a game, the publisher is asking one question: if we put money, people and reputation behind this title, will it reach enough players and earn enough to justify the investment? The answer comes from a structured look at the game, its audience, its data and its costs.

Knowing how that evaluation works helps studios prepare better pitches, spot weak points early and choose partners whose strengths match their game. Below are the areas most publishers examine, followed by a scorecard you can use on your own title.

## Audience fit

Publishers start with who the game is for and whether those players can be reached.

- Is the target audience clearly defined by genre taste, platform, region and age?
- Does that audience exist at meaningful scale in the territories being considered?
- Does the publisher already have channels, community or know-how to reach them?

A strong game for an audience the publisher cannot reach is still a poor fit for that publisher. This is why the same title may be rejected by one partner and welcomed by another.

## Retention

Retention is often the most important signal before launch. It shows whether players come back after their first session, which matters more than how many install.

Publishers typically look at early retention (the next day), medium-term retention (around a week) and longer-term retention (around a month), along with session length and frequency. They compare these against what they have seen in similar games.

If you do not yet have live data, a playtest or small soft launch can provide early indicators. Be open about sample sizes and how players were recruited.

> **Hypothesis to validate:** Publishers weight retention above monetisation at the pre-launch stage because monetisation can be tuned later, while weak retention is harder to fix. Ask each publisher you speak to how they weigh the two.

## Monetisation

Publishers want to understand how the game earns and whether the model suits the target market.

- What is the model: premium, in-app purchases, ads, subscription or hybrid?
- What share of players pay, and how much do they spend?
- Does the model fit local payment habits and price sensitivity?

Market context shapes expectations. In India, for example, Lumikai's CY25 report (via Outlook Respawn) put payer conversion at 25% and showed mid-core ARPPU at $15 against $3 for casual. It also put rewarded ad eCPM at $2.43 against an aggregate ad eCPM of $1.1. Figures like these help publishers judge whether a game's monetisation plan is realistic for a territory.

## Product quality

Publishers play the game. They look at:

- **Stability.** Crashes, bugs and loading issues.
- **Performance.** Frame rate, load times and behaviour on target devices.
- **Core loop.** Whether the main activity is satisfying and repeatable.
- **Onboarding.** Whether new players understand what to do and why.
- **Polish.** Art, audio, UI and overall consistency.

They also judge how much work remains before launch, and whether the studio can deliver it on time.

## Competition

Every game competes for attention. Publishers will ask:

- Which games are closest to yours, and how do you differ?
- Is the genre crowded or underserved in the target market?
- Is there a clear reason a player would choose your game?

A sharp, honest answer to the last question carries more weight than a long list of features.

## Localisation needs

For international launches, publishers estimate how much work is needed to make the game feel local:

- Languages required for interface, tutorials, store listing and support
- Cultural review of art, themes, characters and events
- Payment integrations and pricing changes
- Device and network optimisation for the target region

The more localisation needed, the higher the cost and the longer the timeline. A game built with localisation in mind from the start is easier to take abroad.

## Publishing costs

Publishers build a cost picture that typically covers:

- User acquisition and launch marketing
- Localisation and quality assurance
- Community management and customer support
- Live ops, events and content updates
- Platform, payment and infrastructure costs

They then compare these costs against expected revenue to estimate payback. Studios that understand these costs can have more realistic conversations about revenue share and recoupment.

## Launch potential

Finally, publishers judge the overall opportunity: how big the game could become, how quickly, and with what risk. They consider timing, such as seasonal moments or competing releases, and whether the game can support live operations for years rather than months.

## Evaluation scorecard

Use this scorecard to assess your own game before you pitch. Score each area from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) and note your evidence. If some areas matter more for your genre or target market, weight them accordingly.

| Area | What publishers look for | Score (1 to 5) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audience fit | Clear, reachable audience at scale in target territories | | |
| Retention | Early, weekly and monthly return rates against comparable games | | |
| Monetisation | Model suited to market; realistic payer and spend assumptions | | |
| Product quality | Stable, performant, clear onboarding, satisfying core loop | | |
| Competition | Clear differentiation in the target market | | |
| Localisation needs | Manageable effort for language, culture, payments and devices | | |
| Publishing costs | Reasonable marketing and operating costs against expected revenue | | |
| Launch potential | Timing, scale and ability to sustain live ops | | |

Add up the scores, applying any weighting you have chosen, for a rough overall picture. The number matters less than the conversation it prompts: low scores show where to focus before you pitch.

## Pre-pitch checklist

- [ ] Target audience is defined by genre, platform, region and age
- [ ] Retention data is available from a playtest or soft launch, with sample sizes stated
- [ ] Monetisation model is explained, with assumptions you can defend
- [ ] Build is stable and performs well on target devices
- [ ] Main competitors are named, with a clear point of difference
- [ ] Localisation needs for each target territory are listed
- [ ] Remaining development work and timeline are realistic
- [ ] Live-ops plan covers at least the first months after launch
- [ ] Scorecard has been completed honestly, with evidence for each score
